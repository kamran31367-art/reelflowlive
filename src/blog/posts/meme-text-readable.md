A meme is a picture with a joke on it, and the joke only works if the words are legible. In a chat preview the image can be 200 pixels wide. That is the size to design for, not the full-size file on your desk.

## Why the classic style works

The familiar format - heavy uppercase text, a dark outline, lines stacked above and below the picture - is a set of readability decisions:

- **Uppercase bold** gives every letter the same height and weight, so small sizes stay sharp.
- **The outline** separates text from any background, light or dark.
- **Wrapping to the image width** keeps lines short, and short lines are read faster.

Removing any of the three makes the meme harder to read at feed size. That is why most tools keep them as defaults.

## Keep the text short

Two short lines beat one long sentence. The top line sets up the situation, the bottom line delivers the turn. If a line needs more than about six words at 1080 pixels wide, it will wrap into a cluttered block or shrink below comfort on a phone.

Test the result where it will be seen: shrink the preview to thumbnail size and check whether the words still land.

## Layout details that matter

- Leave a margin between the text and the image edge - cropping happens.
- Choose the anchor on the image where the text does not cover the subject's face.
- A light picture under white text needs a stronger outline, not just a shadow.

## Use images you have the right to use

Making a meme does not change who owns the photo. A picture from a search result or a stock site still belongs to someone. Use your own shots, images licensed for reuse, or platform-provided content. For a private joke between friends the risk is small; for a post on a channel that earns money it is not.

## Checklist

- Two short lines, uppercase, outlined.
- Text inside a margin, away from faces and edges.
- Checked at thumbnail size, not just full size.
- Image rights considered before publishing.
